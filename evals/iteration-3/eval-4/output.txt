Hook (0-4s):
Billing by the hour punishes you every time you get better at your craft.

Story (4-68s):
Early in running my freelance video studio, I billed every client by the hour because it felt fair and transparent. Soon a backward incentive took over my business. The faster and sharper I got at editing, the less money I made on every single project. One quarter, I invested weeks learning faster workflows so my clients wouldn't have to wait as long. Right after that, I delivered a launch cut to a repeat client in half the usual time. Their campaign performed better than anything I had made for them before. Even so, the invoice I sent was half my normal pay because the edit took half the hours. Meanwhile, slower freelancers around me were billing double for weaker work simply because they took longer at the timeline. Sending that invoice broke hourly billing for me for good. On my very next proposal, I dropped my timesheet completely. Instead, I quoted one flat project fee tied to the deliverables and turnaround. The client approved that proposal the same afternoon without questioning my hours once.

Turn (68-98s):
Hourly billing puts you and your client on opposite sides of the clock. It penalizes you for mastering your craft and trains clients to audit your timesheet instead of valuing the result you create. Serious clients don't want to buy slow hours in a chair. They want a strong outcome delivered reliably, backed by the judgment you spent years building so their project succeeds faster.

Takeaway (98-115s):
On your next client proposal, take your hourly rate off the page completely. Replace it with one flat project price tied to clear deliverables and turnaround.

Closer (115-120s):
Price the value of the finished work, never the hours on the clock.
